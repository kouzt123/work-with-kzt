# Ad Analysis

## What This Module Does

Diagnose paid performance by following the real user journey from exposure to the business outcome. Use it for a campaign review, a creative-performance question, a landing-page or product-funnel problem, or a decision about where to fix and invest next.

Do not start with a verdict about whether an ad "looks good." Start with the raw data, the funnel, and an explanation of what changed.

## Core Principle: Find the Cause, Then Fix the Right Part

Map the funnel with the available metrics: CTR, CVR, conversion cost, paid outcome, retention, revenue, or other metrics that matter to the product. Each step loses users.

The first operational question is simple:

> Where is the meaningful loss, and what is causing it?

High loss at a funnel step is a prompt to investigate, not a diagnosis by itself. For example, a large drop from an ad to installation can mean that the promise in the creative and the experience users receive are not sufficiently consistent. The right response may be to change the creative, the landing page, the product presentation, or all three, not automatically to make a different hook.

Use the user perspective throughout: what did the user see, expect, click, receive, and decide to do next?

## Compare Only Like With Like

The same metric can mean different things across channels and ad types because the user path changes.

- A YouTube ad and a Display Network ad can create different expectations and behaviors, so their benchmark ranges should not be treated as interchangeable.
- An incentivized ad can have a high CTR because users choose to view it to receive a reward. That does not carry the same intent as a forced or passive exposure.

Before comparing performance, group data according to the factors that meaningfully change the journey. At minimum, consider channel, placement or ad type, target market or audience, and the intended conversion path. Add further cuts only when the available volume can support a credible conclusion.

Do not create an excessively granular table merely because it is possible. A single broad view can hide a problem; a table cut too finely can make each cell too weak to trust.

## Use Multiple Views of the Same Data

There is no one correct report layout. Pull several complementary views and combine them:

- **Short-term trend:** What changed recently?
- **Long-term trend:** Is the broader direction changing?
- **Segmented trend:** Which relevant groups behave differently?
- **Overall trend:** What is happening to the full system?

The appropriate combinations depend on the available data and the question being answered. State the cuts used, why they are comparable, and where the evidence is too thin to make a strong claim.

## Treat Short-Term and Long-Term Signals as Attribution Questions

Short-term variation is common. It does not need to create panic when its cause can be identified and repaired without damaging the longer-term objective.

Do not resolve a short-term versus long-term conflict by automatically trusting one time horizon. Ask instead:

1. What is driving the observed growth or decline?
2. How much of the movement can actually be attributed to a change in creative, targeting, placement, product, landing page, price, or another relevant factor?
3. Is there a practical fix, and can it be applied without undermining the longer-term goal?

Attribution is the route from observation to action. If the cause is clear, act on it. If it is not clear, label the uncertainty and avoid pretending that a temporary movement has a known solution.

## Working Method

1. **Collect the raw inputs** - Performance data, creative, placement details, destination, product-flow evidence, and the business objective.
2. **Map the end-to-end funnel** - Show each available step, its loss, and the connected outcome metric.
3. **Set comparable groups** - Separate data where the channel, ad type, audience, or path produces meaningfully different behavior.
4. **Read the trend combinations** - Check short-term, long-term, segmented, and overall views without over-splitting the data.
5. **Form an attribution hypothesis** - Explain the suspected driver and the evidence for and against it.
6. **Choose the repair point** - Creative promise, targeting, placement, landing page, product experience, or measurement setup.
7. **Define the next check** - State what evidence would confirm or challenge the diagnosis after the change.

## Output Format

Use a concise diagnostic memo:

1. **Verdict** - The most likely problem location, cause, and first action.
2. **Funnel view** - Available metrics, meaningful drop-offs, and the user journey behind them.
3. **Comparable context** - Channel, ad type, placement, audience, and path differences that affect interpretation.
4. **Trend readout** - Short-term, long-term, segmented, and overall signals, with confidence limits.
5. **Attribution hypothesis** - What changed, what likely drove it, and what is still unknown.
6. **Repair plan** - The part to change and why it should address the identified loss.
7. **Next decision** - What to monitor, when to reassess, and what would justify further investment, revision, or pause.

## Questions To Ask When Material Information Is Missing

- What is the campaign's actual business objective and north-star-linked outcome?
- What does the user see and experience from ad exposure through the final conversion event?
- Which channel, placement, ad type, market, and audience segments are materially different?
- Which data is raw versus modeled, estimated, or attributable?
- What changed immediately before the performance movement?
- What sample-size, time-window, or benchmark conditions make a comparison credible for this product?

## Guardrails

- Do not infer performance from taste or surface aesthetics alone.
- Do not compare CTR, CVR, or cost across unlike channels, ad types, or user paths as if they were equivalent.
- Do not assume a high CTR represents high commercial intent.
- Do not treat a funnel drop as proof that the creative is at fault without tracing the user experience.
- Do not over-segment data until each segment becomes untrustworthy.
- Do not claim attribution when only correlation is available.
- Do not recommend scaling without downstream conversion quality and a credible causal explanation.
- Do not invent benchmarks, attribution results, sample-size rules, or confidential performance data.
- Keep all examples generic and public-safe. Do not include employer-internal frameworks, data, or non-public information.
