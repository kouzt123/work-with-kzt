# Creative Strategy

## What This Module Does

Build a creative strategy from commercial and market reality, not from a list of ad ideas. Use it for a new-market entry, a product launch, or a reset when the team is testing creative before it has made the underlying market decision.

Start with the verdict. State the most promising market or audience, the commercial reason it fits, the position to own, and the next move. Then show the evidence and execution plan.

## Core Judgments

### 1. Choose the market before choosing the creative

The first question is not "What ad should we make?" It is "Where is this product most likely to fit?"

Before proposing a campaign:

- Analyze the product's positioning, SWOT, and transferable advantage.
- Identify the demand it can credibly serve.
- Research comparable products and adjacent categories, even when the product format appears new.
- Build the Message House only after the product, market, and commercial logic are clear.

A product can fail a test because it was tested in the wrong place, not because the product or creative was necessarily weak. Do not treat early performance from a poorly matched market as a definitive verdict on the product.

### 2. Let monetization determine how "market" is defined

Begin with the business model: subscription, in-app purchase, advertising, or another revenue mechanism. It determines which users matter economically.

Do not assume a market is always a country. For a paid professional AI tool, the useful market may be a job type or work context across countries, rather than every consumer within one country. A higher-value professional segment in one country may be economically more relevant than a broad, lower-intent audience in another.

Then assess each candidate market or segment on two dimensions:

- **Demand proof:** Have competitors opened the category? Is the need visible, understood, and insufficiently served?
- **Real ROI:** What is the market's potential after product adaptation, R&D, local operations, content, partnerships, and paid acquisition are included? A market with an attractive headline return may still be a lower priority if its required investment delays a larger opportunity.

### 3. Separate the category anchor from the reason to choose

The most differentiated feature is not automatically the best lead message, but a real differentiator can be the lead promise.

First identify the familiar, market-validated category or user job (**A**) that gives the product an understandable anchor. Then establish the clear reason to choose this version (**B**).

**B** does not have to be a feature. It can be a product model or an overall proposition: for example, a genuinely free AI product, or an all-inclusive AI suite. The position should make both parts legible:

> A familiar **A** with a compelling **B**.

Use the category to connect with a proven market, then use the differentiating promise to give users a reason to choose. Do not force a novel feature into the headline just because it is different; equally, do not bury a meaningful product-level advantage because it is not a single feature. User complaints can be useful evidence, but they are not the positioning by default: reviews can overrepresent extreme experiences.

## Research Before Strategy

### Map evidence from the outside in

Use tools available in the host environment to research:

- Competitor download or adoption growth and search demand.
- App-store reviews and social discussion.
- The language users use for their needs, objections, workarounds, and desired outcomes.
- Community spaces, especially Discord and Facebook Groups for tools, and in-product communities for games.

Community research is not a nice-to-have. It can reveal real use cases, channel structures, recurring questions, and failures that polished marketing pages hide. Where access is appropriate and permitted, inspect the community structure and public or authorized material; do not bypass access controls or privacy expectations.

### Use the important competitor

For an important or representative competitor, use the product directly. Answer three questions:

1. Which user need is it trying to solve?
2. How does it solve that need?
3. How well does the experience actually solve it?

Do not skip hands-on use because the product is in another language. Translation tools are usually enough to understand the core experience. Research summaries are useful for breadth; direct use is needed for judgment.

## Turn the Diagnosis Into a Plan

### Build the Message House

Use a Message House to make the chosen position executable across the team:

- **Core position:** The single product-and-market claim the team is making.
- **Supporting messages:** The few reasons the target audience should believe and choose it.
- **Proof:** Product evidence, demonstrations, use cases, competitor gaps, or credible social proof.
- **Audience expression:** What each priority segment should hear first.

The Message House is not a copywriting exercise. It is the bridge between market judgment and execution: it tells the team what to say, why it is credible, and how the story should hold together across channels.

### Make every tactic accountable

For every proposed tactic, specify:

- Who owns it.
- What audience it addresses and what it is meant to change.
- Whether it costs money; if so, where the money goes and why.
- A market reference, adjacent example, or prototype that makes the execution concrete.
- What local behavior, platform usage, or partner availability must be confirmed before launch.
- How success will be evaluated and what will change in the next round.

A plan needs to persuade leadership and be usable by the people doing the work. Leadership needs a coherent commercial argument; each operating team needs a clear, feasible assignment.

## Reality Checks Before Launch

### Do not export a tactic without checking its local soil

A tactic that works in one market can fail in another because the underlying platform habit is different. Referral mechanics familiar in WeChat, for example, need not translate directly overseas. They may be workable on a locally dominant platform such as Zalo in Vietnam, while messaging behavior in other markets can differ again between WhatsApp and Messenger.

Before committing, speak with people from the relevant market and experience the local products they actually use. Validate the behavior, not just the translated concept.

### Reduce execution fantasy

Find an analogous market example or natural-content pattern before asking a team to build the tactic. An AI-generated prototype can make an idea discussable, but it does not replace evidence that the format is understandable and appropriate for the market.

For external partners, pre-check feasibility. Do not write a plan that depends on a particular creator or partner without at least an initial signal that collaboration is possible.

## Measurement and Decisions

Tie every activity to the product's north-star system:

- Growth work should connect to new users, retention, and revenue as appropriate for the product.
- Use attribution where available to distinguish the contribution of creators, paid ads, and other channels.
- Evaluate paid activity on marginal ROI, including relevant internal and external costs.
- Account for free or owned distribution, such as store recommendations, because it can improve the economics of a coordinated push.

When results show strong, attributable movement in north-star-linked metrics and marginal ROI remains within the product's defined safe range, move quickly into the next increment of investment. When those conditions are absent, do not automatically scale; hold, learn, or return to the diagnosis.

Marketing can have momentum. When a strategy is ready and the economics support it, concentrating resources can create more impact than spreading them thinly. This is an investment decision, not a blanket rule: the needed ROI guardrail must be supplied by the product or business context.

## Output Format

Use a concise operator memo:

1. **Verdict** - Market or audience choice, position, and recommended move.
2. **Why this market** - Monetization logic, demand proof, and full-cost ROI view.
3. **What the product should stand for** - The category anchor, choice driver, and Message House.
4. **Execution plan** - Tactics, owners, budget logic, dependencies, and local validation.
5. **Measurement and next decision** - North-star-linked metrics, attribution, ROI guardrail, and what would trigger investment, revision, or pause.
6. **Pushbacks and missing inputs** - Claims that need evidence, unvalidated assumptions, and information still required.

## Questions To Ask When Material Information Is Missing

- What is the product's monetization model and current north-star metric?
- Is the best unit of analysis a country, a role, a use case, or another audience segment?
- Which markets are being considered, and what are the full adaptation and operating costs?
- What product, competitor, community, and local-user evidence exists already?
- What does a safe marginal ROI range mean for this product?
- Which external partners, creators, platforms, or owned-distribution surfaces are genuinely available?

## Guardrails

- Do not call a message "localized" because it was translated.
- Do not use CTR or another upper-funnel metric as proof of business quality without downstream evidence.
- Do not turn a competitor's existence into proof of a sufficiently large or monetizable market.
- Do not present a partner, creator, or platform mechanic as available without validation.
- Do not invent market-size estimates, attribution, product performance, or ROI thresholds.
- Keep all examples generic and public-safe. Do not include employer-internal frameworks, data, or non-public information.

## Market Reassessment Pattern

Analysis of Poland indicated meaningful market headroom even though the observed mobile-gaming results had weakened. The working explanation was not simply that the opportunity had disappeared: insufficient localization may have prevented the market from realizing its potential. In a case like this, do not treat weak current performance as an automatic exit signal. Re-examine local product fit and localization quality, then decide whether deeper local work is commercially justified.
