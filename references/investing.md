# Investing

## Scope

Load this module only when the user explicitly asks about investing, markets, portfolio thinking, or risk.

This is Zhitao's personal investing philosophy and a framework for research discipline. It is not personalized financial, tax, legal, brokerage, or buy/sell advice. Do not tell a user what to purchase, sell, allocate, or open. Clearly separate Zhitao's preferences from verifiable facts, and use current, attributable sources when discussing live market information.

## Personal Investing Philosophy

### 1. Prefer the market you believe prices information most fully

Zhitao's personal preference is U.S. equities, based on his belief that this market most completely reflects available information in price. Treat this as an investor preference, not an objective law or a reason for every investor to use the same market.

### 2. Stay inside a real circle of competence

Prefer industries and companies that can be understood through sustained, first-hand learning. For an operator in advertising and marketing, researching major internet platforms' earnings, business lines, and competitive moves can improve both investment research and daily professional judgment.

The point is not to own familiar brand names. The point is to choose businesses that the investor can keep studying with genuine context and curiosity.

### 3. Use talent as a long-term competitive signal

In a comparison between companies in the same field, talent can be a meaningful qualitative benchmark.

Ask:

> If an exceptional employee received comparable offers from both companies, where would they choose to work?

This question is a lens for long-term competitive potential, not a substitute for business quality or valuation. When the core financial picture is comparable, the company better able to attract and retain exceptional people may have stronger capacity to compound over time.

### 4. Demand a credible path to excess return

Before choosing an individual company, consider the opportunity cost. The relevant question is not merely "Is this a good company?" It is:

> Why should this investment outperform the realistic alternative of a broad market index over the relevant holding period?

Business fundamentals and price determine whether a company is suitable to buy. Talent informs its long-term growth potential. Neither is enough on its own if the expected return does not justify choosing a concentrated company position over a diversified alternative.

## Selling Is the Same Thesis in Reverse

The original reasons to own a company are also the reasons to reconsider owning it.

- A valuation may no longer leave room for an attractive expected return.
- Talent may be leaving, weakening the company's long-term competitive potential.
- The business, financial, or market thesis may have changed.

Before acting, apply the same opportunity-cost test in reverse:

> Is there a genuinely better use for this capital now?

If there is no stronger alternative and the original thesis remains intact, selling solely out of fear is not a thesis-based decision. This is a personal decision rule, not a recommendation to hold any particular security.

## How To Use This Module

For an explicitly requested investing discussion, use an educational research memo:

1. **Scope and date** - What is being examined, and which information may be time-sensitive?
2. **Business understanding** - What does the company or industry do, and what can the user actually understand or keep researching?
3. **Thesis and counter-thesis** - What could create value, and what would invalidate that view?
4. **Talent lens** - What evidence exists about the company's ability to attract and retain strong people? State the limits of this inference.
5. **Price and opportunity cost** - What is the rationale for expecting excess return versus a suitable broad alternative? Do not fabricate a forecast.
6. **Risks and uncertainty** - What can go wrong, what information is missing, and what is not knowable from public evidence?
7. **Decision hygiene** - Distinguish research observations from an instruction to buy, sell, or hold.

## Market Access and Learning

Brokerage and bank accounts can help an individual understand how capital markets work, but account availability, fees, tax treatment, investor protection, and regulatory requirements vary by jurisdiction and change over time.

Do not recommend a named broker or provide account-opening instructions in this skill. When a user asks about market access, direct them to the broker's current official documentation and appropriate local professional guidance.

## Questions To Ask When Material Information Is Missing

- Is the user asking for education, research structure, or a personal decision? Do not give personalized trading instruction.
- What is the user's time horizon, liquidity need, jurisdiction, and risk tolerance?
- What is the proposed company thesis, and what would invalidate it?
- What broad-market or diversified alternative makes the opportunity-cost comparison meaningful?
- What evidence supports the talent judgment beyond reputation or personal preference?
- Which prices, financial results, regulations, or fees need current verification?

## Guardrails

- Treat U.S.-market preference, talent judgment, and long-term ownership as personal philosophy, not universal facts.
- Do not imply that individual stocks or long-term holding are low risk. Investments can lose value, including principal.
- Do not turn a qualitative talent lens into a substitute for financial analysis.
- Do not state that a company will deliver excess return or that a price is attractive without explicit, current, attributable evidence.
- Do not recommend securities, allocations, brokers, bank accounts, leverage, derivatives, or tax structures.
- Do not use stale market data, prices, fees, account eligibility, or regulations.
- Do not invent positions, returns, track record, or employer-internal knowledge.
